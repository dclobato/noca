Interactive problems (custom validators)

Some problems have no fixed expected output. Instead of comparing your output against a stored answer file, the problem author supplies a program — the custom validator — and the judge runs it side by side with your solution: everything your program prints is read by the validator, and everything the validator prints is fed back to your program's input. The two exchange messages until the conversation ends. Problem statements of such problems describe the protocol you must follow.

Interactive problems still have test cases, and your solution is run against each of them in turn. A test case does not hold an expected answer — it holds the data the validator itself is given before it starts talking to you (the secret number, the hidden graph, the query budget). So each test case is a different round of the same game, and your program plays it from the start. The samples on the statement show you that data for the rounds the author chose to make public; the rest are hidden, just like on any other problem.

Each round ends with the validator deciding how it went: whether your answers were correct, wrong, badly formatted, or whether you took too many queries or too much time for the budget the problem allows. If a round is accepted, the judge moves on to the next one; the first round you do not pass ends the judgment and becomes your verdict — you get Accepted only when every round passes. The remaining verdicts still come from the judging environment itself, which enforces compilation, memory, output and process limits and detects a crashing program exactly as it does on regular problems. A crash on your side always wins over whatever the validator was about to report.

On a submission that was not accepted, the submission page shows you the conversation from the round that failed, so you can read the exchange exactly as it happened.

A buffered message that never reaches the validator deadlocks the attempt.
