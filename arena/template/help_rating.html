Where your rating comes from

Every difficulty and every rating in {{ brand_name }} is read off the record of who attempted what and how many tries it took. Nothing here is set by hand.

{# ── The thesis: the board the numbers are read from ── #}
One problem, as the rating worker sees it Illustrative figures
Attempted by a 318
Solved by s 121
Tries before solving T 407
Difficulty d 6.2

That is the whole input. people attempted the problem, of them solved it, and between them they spent submissions getting there. The difficulty is computed from those three counts and nothing else — not from who solved it, not from when, and not from anyone's opinion of how hard it ought to be.

{# ── Problem difficulty ── #}

How hard is a problem?

Difficulty runs from 0.1 (very easy) to 10.0 (very hard). It rises when few people solve a problem and when solvers need many tries. A new problem with almost no attempts sits near the middle of the scale on purpose, and moves out as evidence arrives.

Once a problem has been attempted enough times, the scale deliberately pushes it toward one end or the other, so the catalogue separates into clearly easy and clearly hard problems instead of collapsing into a mass of average ones.

Each curve is one problem's separation strength. A problem with few attempts follows the straight line and stays near the middle; as attempts accumulate the curve steepens, pulling scores just below the centre toward easy and those just above toward hard.

{{ render_icon(icon="chevron_right") }}The exact computation

Step 1 — Bayesian solve rate

Let $a$ = unique users who attempted the problem, $s$ = unique users who solved it, $\alpha = {{ alpha | int }}$ (prior weight), and $p_0 = {{ prior_solve_rate }}$ (prior solve rate):

$$\hat{p} = \frac{s + \alpha \, p_0}{a + \alpha}$$

Step 2 — Bayesian average tries

Let $T$ = total submission attempts by solvers before their first accepted solution, $\beta = {{ beta | int }}$ (prior weight), and $t_0 = {{ prior_tries | int }}$ (prior average tries):

$$\bar{t} = \frac{T + \beta \, t_0}{s + \beta}$$

Step 3 — Raw central estimate

The two signals are weighted by $w_p = {{ "%.2f" | format(w_solve_rate) }}$ and $w_t = {{ "%.2f" | format(w_tries) }}$, capping the tries component at $T_{\max} = {{ max_relevant_tries | int }}$, into a raw estimate $r \in [0, 1]$:

$$r = w_p \,(1 - \hat{p}) + w_t \,\frac{\ln \bar{t}}{\ln T_{\max}}$$

Step 4 — Bimodal contrast

The raw estimate is pushed toward the extremes with a logistic contrast curve. Two of its parameters are gated by the number of attempts $a$, so a problem with little data stays near the centre and only well-attempted problems are driven outward: the slope (the gain $g$) and the pivot it is measured against (the effective pivot $m_{\text{eff}}$).

The gain rises from 1 toward $g_{\max} = {{ contrast_gain_max | int }}$ with scale $N = {{ contrast_gain_scale | int }}$:

$$g(a) = 1 + (g_{\max} - 1)\bigl(1 - e^{-a/N}\bigr)$$

The effective pivot ramps from the neutral centre $m_0$ (so an unknown problem maps to the middle of the scale) toward the population median raw $m$ (so the easy/hard split stays balanced once there is data), with scale $N_p = {{ pivot_blend_scale | int }}$:

$$\tau(a) = 1 - e^{-a/N_p}$$
$$m_{\text{eff}}(a) = m_0 + \tau(a)\,(m - m_0)$$

With $\operatorname{logit}(x) = \ln \frac{x}{1 - x}$, the raw estimate is then contrasted against the effective pivot:

$$c = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-g\,(\operatorname{logit} r \,-\, \operatorname{logit} m_{\text{eff}})}}$$

A gain of 1 leaves the distribution unchanged; a gain above 1 hollows out the centre. A problem whose raw equals its effective pivot $m_{\text{eff}}$ maps to the middle of the scale — and because the effective pivot sits at the neutral centre when attempts are few, low-data problems land near 5.0 rather than inheriting an easy or hard rating from a skewed population.

Step 5 — Final difficulty

The contrasted value is mapped to an internal integer $d_{\text{int}} \in [1, 100]$, then divided by 10 to produce the display value $d \in [0.1, 10.0]$:

$$d_{\text{int}} = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\max\!\left(1,\;\min\!\left(100,\;1 + 99\,c\right)\right)\right)$$
$$d = d_{\text{int}} / 10$$

{# ── User rating ── #}

What is my rating?

Your rating is the sum of points for every problem you have solved. Harder problems pay exponentially more, so one hard problem can be worth many easy ones.

Because the sum is rebuilt from scratch every cycle using current difficulties, your rating can move without you submitting anything. If a problem you solved gets easier as more people solve it, the points it pays you fall with it. That is working as intended: your rating tracks what your solved set is worth today, not what it was worth the day you solved it.

{{ render_icon(icon="chevron_right") }}The exact computation

Points per problem

Points are computed from the internal integer difficulty $d_{\text{int}} \in [1, 100]$, with base points $B = {{ base_points | int }}$ and growth factor $G = {{ growth }}$. Dividing the exponent by 11 rescales the internal $[1, 100]$ range onto the exponent range $[0, 9]$, so every problem pays exactly what it would on the $[0.1, 10.0]$ display scale:

$$\operatorname{pts}(d_{\text{int}}) = B \cdot G^{(d_{\text{int}} - 1) / 11}$$

This yields {{ pts_difficulty_1 }} points for difficulty 0.1 ($d_{\text{int}} = 1$) and approximately {{ pts_difficulty_10 }} points for difficulty 10.0 ($d_{\text{int}} = 100$) — about {{ pts_ratio }}× more for the hardest problems.

Total user rating

Summing over every problem $p$ you have solved — using each problem's internal difficulty $d_{\text{int}}(p)$ — and rounding to the nearest integer:

$$U = \operatorname{round}\!\left(\sum_{p \,\in\, \text{solved}} \operatorname{pts}\bigl(d_{\text{int}}(p)\bigr)\right)$$
{# ── Confidence ── #}

What does confidence mean?

Confidence says how much of a track record a rating rests on — 0 % with no data, approaching 100 % once someone has solved enough problems for the number to settle. It is not a measure of skill, and a low confidence does not mean a rating is wrong.

It is shown next to ratings on leaderboards so a rating built from three solved problems is not read the same way as one built from three hundred.

{{ render_icon(icon="chevron_right") }}The exact computation

Let $n$ be the number of problems solved and $k = {{ confidence_scale }}$ the saturation scale:

$$C(n) = \operatorname{round}\!\left(100 \cdot \left(1 - e^{-n/k}\right)\right)$$

The function grows quickly and then flattens: around $n = {{ confidence_scale }}$ solved problems confidence reaches ~63 %, and it exceeds 95 % only after roughly $3k \approx {{ confidence_scale * 3 }}$ solved problems.

{# ── Affiliation ── #}

How is an affiliation rated?

An affiliation's rating is a weighted average of its members' ratings, with the strongest member counting most and each next member counting less. A group of consistently strong members outranks a group carrying one standout.

The weights always sum to less than 1, so an affiliation can never be rated above its best member.

{{ render_icon(icon="chevron_right") }}The exact computation

Let $s_0 \ge s_1 \ge \cdots \ge s_{n-1}$ be the member scores sorted in descending order, and let {% if affiliation_factor %} $f = {{ affiliation_factor }}$ {% else %} $f$ {% endif %} be the decay factor:

$$S = \frac{1}{f} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \left(1 - \frac{1}{f}\right)^{\!i} s_i$$

The weights sum to 1 as $n \to \infty$, so the affiliation rating is always bounded by the top member's score. A larger decay factor $f$ causes the weights to decrease more slowly, meaning more members contribute meaningfully.

{# ── Cadence ── #}

When do the numbers change?

{% if rating_interval_text %} Everything on this page is recomputed every {{ rating_interval_text }}, in one batch. {% else %} Everything on this page is recomputed in one batch, on the cadence published by the rating worker. {% endif %} Nothing updates the moment you submit.

The three stages always run in this order, so each one sees the freshest output of the one before it:

  1. Problem difficulty, from attempts and solves.
  2. User ratings, from the difficulties just written.
  3. Affiliation ratings, from the user ratings just written.
